Use only lenses relevant to the research question. These are prompts for evidence-based analysis, not a checklist to fill mechanically.

## Character construction

Ask:

- What is the audience/player's first usable model of the character?
- Which later evidence confirms, complicates, or contradicts that model?
- What does the character want, fear, hide, misunderstand, or refuse to admit?
- Which traits are stated, which are demonstrated, and which are inferred from consequences?
- How does the work create competence, vulnerability, contradiction, agency, and cost?
- What information is delayed, and what changes when it arrives?
- Does the character change, reveal an already-existing self, or force others to change?

Failure modes: trait lists without scene evidence; calling inconsistency “complexity”; attributing audience sympathy to creator intent without evidence.

## Relationships and intimacy

Ask:

- What is exchanged first: utility, trust, vulnerability, status, information, or risk?
- Which actions create earned trust rather than declared closeness?
- What boundaries, conflicts, or asymmetries prevent the relationship from becoming frictionless?
- How do optional interactions, shared space, silence, gifts, routines, or callbacks accumulate meaning?
- What changes after betrayal, disclosure, sacrifice, or dependency?

Look for progression mechanisms, not only romance labels or friendship milestones.

## Plot and dramatic structure

Ask:

- What question/tension is active at each phase?
- What new information changes the meaning of previous events?
- Where do reversals alter goals, stakes, allegiance, or interpretation?
- How are anticipation, delay, payoff, and aftermath distributed?
- Which decisions create irreversible consequences?
- Where does the work intentionally lower intensity to create contrast?

Do not reduce every plot to a fixed three-act template unless evidence supports that framing.

## Interactive narrative and quest design

Ask:

- What can the player choose, and what is merely presented as choice?
- What information is available before the choice?
- Are consequences immediate, delayed, systemic, relational, cosmetic, or epistemic?
- Does gameplay reinforce or contradict the narrative claim?
- How are alternate routes authored without destroying pacing or characterization?
- Which constraints come from production rather than narrative theory?

Separate authored branches from emergent player stories.

## Worldbuilding

Ask:

- Which institutions shape daily behavior?
- How are economics, technology, law, violence, class, religion, media, and infrastructure made visible?
- What does the world imply through routine rather than exposition?
- Which details have causal relationships and which are decorative texture?
- What contradictions reveal power structures or social conflict?

Prefer systems and relationships over encyclopedic lore accumulation.

## Environmental storytelling

Ask:

- What can be inferred from space, object placement, signage, architecture, sound, advertisements, wear, bodies, or traces of activity?
- Which environmental details change interpretation when combined?
- Is the player required to notice them or are they optional depth?
- Does the environment communicate past events, present systems, or future threat?

Distinguish designed evidence from accidental visual coincidence.

## Exposition and information release

Ask:

- Who knows what, and when?
- Why is information delivered at this moment and through this medium?
- Is exposition embedded in conflict, action, environment, dialogue, documents, UI, or optional exploration?
- What cognitive load is required before the information becomes meaningful?
- Which terms are introduced before explanation to create curiosity?

## Theme and motif

Ask:

- Which recurring choices, images, institutions, consequences, or conflicts support the proposed theme?
- Does the theme emerge from multiple systems or only from dialogue?
- Are there meaningful counterexamples that complicate the reading?

Treat themes as interpretations unless creators explicitly state them.

## Scene construction and perspective

Ask:

- Where is the player/viewer positioned physically and informationally?
- Who controls attention?
- What can be inspected, interrupted, ignored, or revisited?
- What does staging communicate without dialogue?
- How does first-person/third-person/camera control affect identification and agency?

## Reception gap

When useful, compare:

- creator-stated goal;
- implemented mechanism;
- critic/community interpretation;
- observed reasons for divergence.

Do not define audience disagreement as design failure without a clear evaluation criterion.
