# Reference-Set Discovery

## Purpose

Build a deliberate set of cultural works to study before designing an original game or narrative project. Optimize for useful mechanisms, contrasting approaches, and source richness rather than popularity or market similarity.

## Start from research axes

Translate the project idea into 3–7 research axes. Useful axes include:

- central theme or ethical tension;
- character archetype or relationship dynamic;
- plot problem or information structure;
- player agency / choice architecture;
- worldbuilding system or institution;
- tone and emotional trajectory;
- spatial or environmental storytelling problem;
- perspective, camera, or interaction constraint.

Avoid starting from genre alone. “Cyberpunk games” produces surface similarity; “stories where intimacy grows under forced cognitive proximity” produces more useful references.

## Search in layers

For each axis:

1. search obvious canonical works to learn vocabulary;
2. search adjacent media for different solutions to the same problem;
3. search creator interviews, talks, postmortems, and production material to verify that the work is researchable;
4. search at least one contrasting or failed/criticized example when useful.

Use model memory only to propose search candidates. Confirm every included work through web sources before adding it to the final set.

## Candidate record

Record each candidate as:

```text
Title:
Medium:
Release/year:
Research axes:
Why it is relevant:
Distinct mechanism to study:
Source richness: high|medium|low
Best known source leads:
Priority: core|secondary|contrast
```

“Why it is relevant” must name a mechanism or research question, not merely a shared genre or visual style.

## Selection rules

Prefer a small set with complementary value over a long recommendation list.

A strong set normally contains:

- **core references** — directly solve the same narrative/design problem;
- **adjacent references** — solve a similar problem in another medium or genre;
- **contrast references** — use a meaningfully different mechanism or expose a failure mode.

Avoid selecting many works that all contribute the same lesson.

## Source-richness check

Before promoting a candidate to core reference, verify that enough web material exists to study it:

- creator interviews or commentary;
- conference talks or postmortems;
- official scripts, codices, setting material, or production notes;
- reputable deep criticism or specialist analysis;
- accessible transcripts or archives.

A brilliant work with almost no accessible production evidence may still be useful, but mark the evidence limitation in advance.

## Ranking

Rank candidates on research value, not commercial success.

Useful dimensions:

- relevance to the research axis;
- uniqueness of mechanism;
- quality of available primary/creator sources;
- contrast value;
- transferability to the user's project constraints.

Do not use sales, review score, player count, or popularity as default ranking signals.

## Output

For each selected work, provide:

1. why it belongs in the set;
2. the exact question to investigate;
3. the mechanism expected to be useful;
4. the first 2–5 source leads worth opening;
5. priority and evidence limitations.

Finish with a **research order** that explains which works should be studied first and what each later work adds that the earlier ones do not.
