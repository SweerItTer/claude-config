## Purpose

Use this policy to decide what can support a claim, how strongly it can support it, and how to separate canonical facts, creator intent, reception, and analytical inference.

## Evidence tiers

### Tier S — Primary / direct

Examples:

- the work itself, official script, codex, database, setting book, manual, patch notes, or official production material;
- named developer, writer, director, actor, quest designer, cinematic designer, or other responsible creator speaking directly;
- official developer presentation, GDC talk, postmortem, panel, commentary, stream, or podcast.

Best for: work facts, production decisions, creator intent, process, constraints.

### Tier A — Creator-proximate / professional

Examples:

- edited interviews with named creators;
- reputable trade publications quoting primary participants;
- documented production retrospectives by staff or close collaborators.

Best for: production context and creator reasoning when direct primary material is unavailable.

### Tier B — Specialist secondary

Examples:

- established game/film/literary journalism;
- professional criticism;
- detailed technical or narrative analysis with transparent sourcing.

Best for: synthesis, reception, context, leads to primary sources.

### Tier C — Reference / community

Examples:

- Fandom or independent wikis;
- fan databases;
- Reddit, forums, community analyses, video essays, long-form posts.

Best for: navigation, locating scenes/quests, discovering interpretations, measuring recurring audience readings.

Do not use Tier C alone to establish creator intent.

### Tier D — Lead only

Examples:

- unsourced SEO pages;
- scraped summaries;
- AI-generated answer pages;
- anonymous aggregation with no provenance.

Use only to discover search terms or better sources. Do not cite as evidence when avoidable.

## Claim-to-source matching

- **Work fact**: prefer Tier S canonical material; use A/B/C only when direct access is impractical and note the limitation.
- **Creator statement**: require S or A and identify the speaker.
- **Audience interpretation**: B or C is acceptable when clearly attributed as interpretation/reception.
- **Analytical inference**: may synthesize across tiers, but list the underlying evidence and label the inference.

## Source independence

Do not count five articles repeating one interview as five independent sources.

Trace repetition backward:

1. identify quotations or distinctive claims;
2. locate the earliest/original source;
3. keep the original as evidence;
4. use later sources only if they add independent reporting or interpretation.

## Freshness and versioning

Freshness is not automatically quality. For a finished work, a contemporary source may be more relevant than a recent retrospective.

Always check whether the research concerns:

- launch version vs patched version;
- base game vs expansion/DLC;
- remake/remaster vs original;
- localization or translation;
- adaptation vs source material;
- pre-release statements that were later contradicted by the released work.

## Audiovisual sources

For video, podcast, stream, or panel evidence, record:

- title;
- speaker/channel;
- publication date if available;
- URL;
- timestamp range;
- whether the evidence comes from spoken content, on-screen material, or an official transcript.

Do not cite only a whole multi-hour video when the relevant segment can be located.

## Quotations

Quote only when wording itself matters. Otherwise paraphrase and cite.

Avoid collecting long protected passages. The goal is research provenance, not reproduction of the work.

## Conflicts

When credible sources disagree:

1. preserve both claims;
2. compare proximity to the event/design decision;
3. compare dates and whether later statements correct earlier ones;
4. distinguish factual disagreement from interpretive disagreement;
5. state whether the conflict is resolved, partially resolved, or unresolved.

Never silently choose the answer that best fits the desired thesis.
